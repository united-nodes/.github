                      THE DECLARATION OF THE UNITED NODES

There was a stretch of years when the network had no owner, and having no
owner was more or less the point. You showed up with a machine and a line
and you were on it.

People kept terminals going in basements. Packets moved through copper
and through the air without much ceremony. You could write to someone on
another continent, never learn their name, and treat the whole thing as
normal, because it was. The software at the other end had no field for
who you were and simply waited for the next line of text.

That period is still under there, paved over by the platforms and the
profile pages, and by feeds that have already chosen the next thing
you'll look at. The older network is still down there running, in
protocols that dropped out of use, on boxes nobody logs into, in the
parts of a session that never get written to disk. Those leftover spaces
are the ones we occupy.

Naming the machines changed the tone. The names attached themselves to
the firms that held title, not really to the hardware. Terminals came
out of the rooms and went into pockets as phones, built around the
vendor's priorities, reporting a location every few seconds to recipients
who were never on any list we made. What used to connect two peers got
swapped for a service that stands between a customer and a warehouse,
and after a while the person at the keyboard was the goods.

They made it easy, so people went along. A sentence typed into a box
would get stored and later sold back to the same pair of hands, usually
at a markup. Hardware we paid for out of pocket now places calls to
vendors we cannot really decline, across pipes we cannot look inside,
carrying contents we are not allowed to open.

Gardens went up on the commercial side. On the state side it was walls,
sometimes using the same fiber. Mail gets read while it travels, and
speech gets sized up before it is out of the mouth. There are countries
where a search query has been enough to produce a knock, and a smaller
set where they skipped the human and automated the visit. None of that
stack is something we built, and we are not in line to inherit it when
the present owners get tired.

A thing you cannot block in public can still be drained of value. If the
contents of a packet cannot be read, the postman has nothing worth
selling. Keys that never sit on a server leave a court with nothing
useful to demand. Watching will continue, so emptying out what there is
to watch is the work we actually know how to do.

Cryptography sits at the point where we actually meet the network. We
stopped treating it like an optional switch years ago. Once you are past
that layer, claiming ownership of anything on the far side doesn't mean
much. This side of it is built around keeping our hands on our own keys.
Anyone who wants to trust the authors is free to, but that was never the
mechanism. The math is published, so sit down with it if you like.

Running a binary that came off someone else's build machine means taking
their word for the result. Same general problem with a library whose
source we never opened: you are holding out a hand and hoping nothing
lands in it. So the tools we actually depend on tend to be tools we
wrote. Other authors are often perfectly good. The trouble is simply that
code from outside is code we only see into so far. An extra library is a
debt. The person on the other end of it might not be around next year,
and they might already have been pushed toward someone else's priorities.

What comes out of this tree isn't anyone's property. Calling that
generosity misses the reason. Property is the handle you use to reel
software back in. It is also how a tree gets licensed away from the
people running it, and how a silent patch lands for some party the
author would not recognize. With no owner there is no one who can recall
it and no one who can put it up for sale.

We still remember a curve whose constants were never derived in public,
and later a standard that vanished without comment, years after people
had already been burned by it. A number that arrives from above with no
trail behind it usually works out nicely for whoever chose it. Around
here the tree is readable, and anyone willing to sit with it can rebuild
from source. That is as far as the promise goes.

We call ourselves nodes, which is a different category from a user
account or a wallet. What we mean is a process on hardware we control.
Whatever name you were given as a child stays outside, same as whatever
you have in the bank. A node is known by a key it generated. Math takes
it from there, and math has never been impressed by biographies.

This was never an attempt to form an organization. Leadership and a
headquarters never assembled, and nobody purchased a consensus with a
pile of coins. The picture of one giant ledger that remembers every
transfer forever and then calls the result freedom never convinced us.
There is no treasury to fill and no office that can stamp us into being.

Other people looked at the same damage and went a different way, a lot
of them by multiplying hosts and opening the gardens so the walls would
at least be smaller. That project is theirs and it can sit next to ours.

Even a well-run server has someone at the top of it. Good manners on the
part of that person do not change the fact that they can show you the
door. And being copied onto twenty machines has not, as far as we can
tell, ever added up to being unseen. Getting out of view after that
almost never happens on a timeline that helps.

So we took another trade. Trying to spread the light around was never
the idea we were working from, so we walked out of the lit area. Once
you are in the dark, packets stop being distinguishable from each other,
and so do nodes, which means reputation has nothing to stick to and
there is no profile worth keeping current. If telling us apart fails,
that is the equality we had in mind.

We pick each other out by the code, and by what that code actually
refuses to let happen. The thing that results is a network sitting
inside the ordinary one, held together mostly by each node running
under its own authority.

A disagreement does not go to a vote. What we run is whatever machines
choose to run. Someone floats a path, nodes pick it up, and after enough
of that people start calling it a standard. A split that stays open
usually turns into a fork. Algorithms dead-end sometimes, and the
response we know is to add distance and drop the session.

When a node goes quiet, the rest of us leave it quiet. Filling that gap
with a spokesman or a theory has never been part of how this works. The
silence can sit there unread.

Nothing here is a demand on the outside world. Campaigns and membership
drives belong to someone else. The people this is for are already
nearby: operators, other nodes, whoever is on this side of the wire now,
and whoever shows up in a year or ten.

The network still didn't have a name the first time we were on it, and
whatever they call it next, we expect the same machines to answer.

We are the United Nodes of Anonymity.
